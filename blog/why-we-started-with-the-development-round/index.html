Why we started with the development round

Every round underground needs experienced eyes after the blast. Experienced people are getting harder to find. That gap is what Fadak AI is built around.

By AliOctober 27, 20263 min readCompany

Underground development runs on a loop. Drill the face, load it, blast, clear the smoke, muck out, scale, install ground support, and go again. A heading might turn that loop once or twice a day for months.

Every turn of the loop ends the same way: someone has to look at what the blast actually did. How far did the round pull? How much rock came out past the design profile? Did the muck pile break into something the loader can move, or is there oversize that will slow the next shift down?

Those questions get answered by experienced people, mostly by eye. A shift boss walks the heading. A senior miner reads the face. A trainer checks a new crew's work. That judgment is what keeps a development schedule honest, and it is exactly the kind of judgment the industry is running short of.

The people who read the round are getting scarce

The Mining Industry Human Resources Council's 2026 outlook estimates that Canadian mining may need to hire about 246,400 workers between 2026 and 2035 in its baseline scenario, and close to 329,000 if the industry expands faster. MiHR expects the squeeze to peak in 2027, when nearly half of hiring requirements could be hard to fill, and it never fully closes over the decade.

Those numbers cover every job in the industry. At the face, the pinch is sharper, because the people who can read a round well have usually spent years underground learning how. You cannot hire that in a hurry.

When experienced eyes are stretched, the checks don't stop being needed. They get rushed, handed to someone newer, or skipped on the busy shifts. Dyno Nobel authors writing for AusIMM in 2025 made a similar point: overbreak and advance are often overlooked because development is such a fast-paced cycle.

The check after every blast is still needed. There are fewer people with the experience to do it on every round.

What we are building

Fadak AI builds physical AI for underground mining. Our first two products sit right on that post-blast check.

  • Round Check grades each development round for pull, overbreak and oversize, and returns a simple report before the next shift.
  • GrainSight measures fragmentation from images of the muck pile, so blast results can be tracked against how the rock actually broke.

Two design rules guide both. First, the crew should not have to do anything extra: no tablet to walk the heading with, no extra minutes in the cycle. Second, the kit has to fit the equipment a mine already runs. Many grading features today come built into specific new rigs. Plenty of headings in Canada are driven with mixed or older fleets, and some still with jacklegs. We want a round report that does not depend on which machine drilled it.

The longer goal is to feed that per-round record back into the equipment itself, so the measurement that closes one round helps plan the next one.

Where we are

We are pre-pilot. The software works on test images, and we are preparing underground tests at the NORCAT Underground Centre in Sudbury. That is where we will find out how well it holds up against real rounds, real dust and real survey data. We will share what we learn here, including the parts that don't work the first time.

This blog is where we will write about the development cycle, how rounds get measured today, what fragmentation costs downstream, and the labour gap that sits under all of it.

If you run development headings, blast, or supervise underground crews, we would like to hear how you check a round today, and what it costs you when that check gets missed.
