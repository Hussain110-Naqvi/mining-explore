What a metre of development really costs

Published Canadian cost data puts a metre of lateral development in the thousands of dollars, and much of that cost is paid per round, not per metre. Here is where a few points of pull show up in the budget and the schedule.

By AliNovember 17, 20265 min readExplainer

Ask a mine planner what development costs and the answer comes back in dollars per metre. Ask a crew what a round costs and they will think in shifts. Both are right, and the gap between them is where pull starts to matter.

The published number

One of the clearest public breakdowns comes from Gary Poxleitner of SRK Consulting (Canada), in a 2024 presentation for CIM based on actual costs from operating mines in British Columbia, Ontario and Quebec. For a single 5 m by 5 m heading in good ground, he puts the all-in operating cost at about C$5,000 per metre. Add maintenance labour and indirects such as admin, training and utilities, and it reaches about C$6,650 per metre.

The breakdown is the useful part. In SRK's good-ground case, operations labour is the largest line at about C$2,000 per metre. Ground support comes next at about C$1,700, split across bolts and screen, shotcrete and cables. Services such as pipe, road and ventilation add about C$400. Explosives and loading add about C$350, mucking about C$300 and jumbo drilling about C$250.

Costmine data points the same way. A 2022 Canadian Mining Journal article by Costmine engineer Sam Blakely put labour at roughly 50% to 60% of the cost of driving drifts, with supplies at about 15% to 20%.

Advance rates set the clock

The other half of the picture is time. In a 2020 Canadian Mining Journal article, CEMI's Doug Morrison described conventional drill-and-blast in a deep Canadian mine as advancing about 3 m a day. SRK's 2024 slides are blunter. The best standard performance they cite is only 4.0 m a day, and they note that sustainable rates have been "decreasing not increasing" over time.

SRK's Northern Ontario case study shows why. A 3.8 m advance took about 25.4 hours of development work per round, and the heading averaged about 1.9 m a day. Of a 12-hour portal-to-portal shift, only about 7.7 hours were operating time.

Where a few points of pull show up

Here is the part that is easy to miss. Several of those cost lines are paid per round, not per metre. A crew drills, loads and blasts the full face whether the round breaks 3.5 m or 3.2 m. The shift costs the same either way. Ground support and mucking scale more with the metres actually broken, so they behave differently.

The numbers below are our own arithmetic on SRK's published figures, not results from any mine. SRK's base case drills 3.6 m and breaks 3.5 m, about 97% pull. Suppose the same heading pulls 89% instead, breaking 3.2 m.

  • Rounds: 1,000 m at 3.5 m a round takes about 286 rounds. At 3.2 m it takes about 313. That is 27 extra rounds.
  • Schedule: at the case study's pace of about two days a round, 27 extra rounds is close to two months on one heading for every kilometre.
  • Cost: if operations labour, drilling and explosives are treated as fixed per round, those lines rise by about 9% per metre. On SRK's figures that adds roughly C$240 to every metre, before counting the time.
A round that pulls short costs about the same as a full one, and delivers less.

Pull is not the only lever. In SRK's poor-ground case, all-in cost rises by about half, driven by doubled operations labour and heavier support. In SRK's figures, ground conditions, heading size and dual-heading work each move cost per metre more than our pull example does. But pull is one of the few levers a crew touches every round.

What published work says about improving it

Rounds can be made to pull better. A 2023 Curtin University thesis by Adam Witt looked at development at a Western Australian underground gold mine in highly laminated ground. Its optimised design added an average of 0.3 m of cut length per round over the operation's average, reduced the spread of cut lengths by 25%, and cut consumables cost by about 15% per metre of advance.

That spread matters as much as the average. A heading that pulls well on most rounds and badly on a few is hard to schedule. It is also hard to fix if nobody records which rounds fell short or why.

Why we care about this

None of these gains can be seen without measuring every round. As we wrote in our post on pull, overbreak and fragmentation, a few points of pull on a short round is a few centimetres. That is too small to judge by eye on a busy shift.
