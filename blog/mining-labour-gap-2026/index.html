The 2026 mining labour gap, and what it means at the face

Canada's mining industry needs a quarter of a million hires in a decade, and the hardest jobs to fill are often the most physical. A look at the numbers and where technology can actually help.

By AliNovember 10, 20263 min readIndustry

Two reports from 2026 tell most of the story about work in Canadian mining right now. One is about how many people the industry needs. The other is about what the work does to the people already doing it.

How many people the industry needs

In June 2026, the Mining Industry Human Resources Council (MiHR) published its Canadian Mining Labour Market Outlook. In its baseline scenario, the industry may need to hire about 246,400 workers between 2026 and 2035, counting both growth and replacements for people who retire or leave. In an expansion scenario, that rises to nearly 329,000.

~246,400hires needed, 2026–2035, baseline
~329,000hires needed in the expansion scenario
+16%workforce growth to about 242,400 people by 2035, baseline

The timing matters as much as the totals. MiHR expects the tightest point in 2027, when nearly half of hiring requirements could be hard to fill. After that, it expects 15% to 25% of hires to stay hard to recruit through most of the decade. In MiHR's forecast, hiring needs run ahead of new entrants every year to 2035. The gap narrows after 2027, but it does not close.

MiHR's polling of young Canadians also found that mining's image is improving, but safety, remote locations and time away from home can still outweigh higher pay and job stability.

What the work asks of people

Workplace Safety North's 2025 snapshot for Ontario mining reported 4.05 injuries per 100 workers in the mining sector and 4.25 in mining pits and quarries. Across both, bodily reaction and exertion, which covers overexertion, repetitive motion and the physical demands of the job, was the leading cause of lost-time injuries.

Put the two reports together and a pattern shows up. The jobs that are hardest on the body are also the ones that are hardest to keep staffed, and the people with the most experience in them are the ones closest to retirement.

What that looks like at the face

Underground development depends on judgment that takes years to build. Reading a blasted round, spotting a bad profile, knowing when ground support is right: these come from time underground, and trainer roles in the industry commonly ask for a decade or more of it.

When those people are stretched across more headings, more crews and more new hires, something gives. Usually it is the checks that don't produce metres directly: the look at the round after the blast, the record of how it broke, the feedback to a newer crew about what went wrong.

A labour shortage underground shows up first in the checks that don't produce metres directly.

Where technology helps, and where it doesn't

Technology will not replace an experienced shift boss. What it can do is make that experience go further:

  • A consistent record of every round, so problems show up in the data instead of depending on who happened to be on shift.
  • Faster feedback for newer crews, so they see the result of their round before the next one, not weeks later.
  • Experienced eyes where they matter most, spent on the rounds that need attention instead of walking every heading to confirm the ones that went fine.

For any of that to help, it has to remove work, not add it. A tool that needs a person to walk the heading with a tablet has to compete with everything else that person is doing.
