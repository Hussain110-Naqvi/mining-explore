The mill is the first place a blast gets judged at scale. Rock that breaks finer at the face moves through crushing and grinding faster. Rock that breaks coarse, or full of hard mid-sized lumps, slows everything down. Mine-to-mill is the name for treating those two ends of the operation as one problem instead of two budgets.

What the mill wants from the blast

Mill people talk about feed in a few terms. P80 is the size that 80% of the material passes, as we covered in our post on pull, overbreak and fragmentation. Fines are the material that is already small enough to need little further work. Critical size is the awkward middle: pieces too small to act as grinding media in a SAG mill and too big to break quickly, so they build up in the charge and limit throughput.

A 2022 paper in Minerals by Homero Delboni Jr. and co-authors from the University of São Paulo and Vale shows how this plays out. They modelled the Sossego plant in Brazil treating harder ore from the Cristalino deposit. Further crushing 35% of the SAG mill's fresh feed lifted modelled circuit capacity by 12%, from 1,570 to 1,758 tonnes an hour. In pilot work, adding a secondary crusher cut SAG specific energy from 7.8 to 6.6 kWh per tonne, about 15%.

The same paper names the catch. Once the SAG mill had finer feed, the ball mill's installed power became the limit. Finer feed moves the bottleneck. It does not remove it.

What published drill-to-mill projects reported

Crushing is one way to get finer feed. The blast is another, and several projects have put numbers on it.

up to 10%throughput gain achieved at Mont Wright, Canada, across ore types (Hatch, 2022)
up to 15%SAG throughput gain from high-energy test blasts at Porgera (AusIMM, 2003)
12%modelled capacity gain from finer SAG feed at Sossego, Brazil (Minerals, 2022)

At ArcelorMittal's Mont Wright iron ore operation, a project run with Hatch simulated throughput gains of 7% to 26% across grinding lines, depending on ore domain. The 2022 paper reports up to 10% achieved overall, with little or no capital spending as the goal.

At the Porgera gold mine in Papua New Guinea, trials run with Dyno Nobel and the Julius Kruttschnitt Mineral Research Centre from 2000 to 2002 found up to 15% more SAG throughput with high-energy blast designs. The abstract also flags concerns about ore dilution and wall damage affecting the economic benefit.

At Newmont's Ahafo mine in Ghana, a Metso process integration study set blast designs to raise the share of ultra-fines in the feed from 18% to 24%. E&MJ reported a significant increase in mill throughput but gave no percentage.

A 2023 Dyno Nobel case study from an unnamed surface metal mine reports at least a 15% increase in mill throughput and up to 10% more material finer than half an inch, after changes to burden, spacing, explosive density, stemming and timing. The customer valued the gain at $58.1 million over a year. That is a vendor case study with the customer's own estimate, and it should be read that way.

The fine print

Three things stand out across this work. First, almost all of it is from open pits, where blasts are large and the link from one blast to the mill feed is easier to trace. Second, more energy in the rock has costs elsewhere: Porgera's dilution and wall damage concerns are the honest version of that. Third, the gains have to be kept.

A 2007 AusIMM paper by A. Dance, W. Valery and colleagues at Metso was written about exactly that problem. A mine-to-mill study produces recommendations, but whether they hold depends on the operation. They ask the question every mill manager should:

"How does an operation know if the benefit continues?"

Their answer is measurement, direct and indirect, because ore and rock properties change over time. A blast design tuned for one domain drifts out of tune as the mine moves.

Underground is harder to trace

Underground, the link between face and mill is real but harder to follow. Rock from many stopes and headings is blended on its way up, and much of the rock from waste development never reaches the mill at all.

The published work exists but is thinner. A 2017 AusIMM Underground Operators' paper by J. Jackson and E. Sellers built blasting indices for an underground open stope mine from diamond drill data. The indices flagged stopes likely to give oversize, underbreak and overbreak, and predicted indicative mill throughput. SRK's Hugo Melo has written about how mill feed P80 shifts as a block cave develops. Both point the same way: knowing what each blast produced is the starting point for anything downstream.

Why we care about this

Every result above depends on knowing the fragmentation of each blast, not a monthly average. GrainSight is built to estimate fragmentation from images of the muck pile, and Round Check grades oversize alongside pull and overbreak on each round. The aim is a per-round record that a blasting engineer and a mill metallurgist can both read.

We are pre-pilot and preparing underground tests at the NORCAT Underground Centre in Sudbury. If your mill tracks feed size, we would like to know whether anyone can trace a bad shift in the mill back to the blast that caused it.
