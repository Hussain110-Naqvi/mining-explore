Every development blast produces three results that matter to the people paying for the metres: how far the round advanced, how cleanly it broke to the design profile, and what size the broken rock ended up. Mining people call these pull, overbreak and fragmentation. Here is what each one means and why it is worth tracking round by round.

1. Pull: did the round advance as far as it was drilled?

Pull, or advance, is the length of rock actually broken compared with the length drilled. Drill 3.6 m and break 3.4 m, and you have pulled about 94%.

The gap matters because every lost centimetre is paid for twice. The holes were drilled and loaded for the full length, and the missing rock still has to come out on a later round. Across a heading that advances every day, a few points of pull add up to weeks of schedule.

Results vary widely. A paper presented at EXPLO 99 found most development rounds in the Australian goldfields it studied pulled between 84% and 94%. Suppliers of modern drilling and initiation systems now claim 95% or better. The distance between those two numbers is where a lot of development cost lives.

Measuring it well is harder than it looks. On a 4-foot (1.22 m) round, an error of ±10 cm is about ±8 points of pull, which is bigger than the improvement most mines are chasing. Useful measurement needs to be closer to ±3 cm, or about ±2.5 points.

2. Overbreak: did the blast stay inside the design?

Overbreak is rock removed beyond the planned excavation profile. Its opposite, underbreak, is rock left inside the profile that has to be drilled or scaled out later.

Overbreak costs in several places at once:

  • more rock to muck and haul that nobody planned to move;
  • more shotcrete or support to fill and secure a larger opening;
  • a more damaged rock mass around the opening, which can make ground control harder.

In contract development, overbreak can also turn into an argument about who pays for the extra metres. A consistent record of each round's profile helps settle that with data instead of memory.

3. Fragmentation: what size did the rock break to?

Fragmentation describes the size spread of the broken rock. It is usually summarized as P80, the size that 80% of the material passes. Too many large blocks, or oversize, slow the loader and may need secondary breaking before the rock can move. The size coming out of the blast also sets up everything downstream.

Downstream is where the energy goes. A 2021 study commissioned by Weir and written by the director of the Coalition for Eco-Efficient Comminution reviewed more than 40 published studies across five commodities. It found crushing and grinding use about 25% of final energy at an average mine site, and about 26% at gold operations. Rock that leaves the face better broken can mean less work in the mill.

Why these aren't measured on every round

None of this is news to mining engineers. Tools exist to measure all three: survey scanners, rig-mounted profilers, and photo-based fragmentation software. The problem is usually not whether something can be measured. It is whether anyone has the time to do it every round.

Most current methods need a person on site, minutes per round, or a specific make of drill rig. In a fast development cycle with a stretched crew, measurement is the step that slips. Dyno Nobel authors writing for AusIMM in 2025 noted that overbreak and advance are often overlooked for exactly that reason.

Measurement also doesn't fix a round on its own. In a Dyno Nobel case study from the George Fisher mine in Australia, the cost and cycle gains came from electronic detonators and changes to perimeter and drill design. Scanning and photogrammetry were how the team proved which changes worked. That is the role we think measurement should play: a consistent record, every round, so the people making blast decisions can see what changed.
